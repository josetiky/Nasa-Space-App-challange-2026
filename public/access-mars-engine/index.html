Marte

SKIP INTRO
This photo taken from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter shows the Bradbury Landing site where you are standing now, along with the Sky Crane descent stage impact and parachute. All the terrain around you has been reconstructed in 3D using stereo Navcam and Mastcam mosaics combined with orbital HiRISE elevation models. Sky Crane thrusters excavated the Goulburn gravel scour here, exposing rounded fluvial pebbles transported by ancient streams in Gale Crater. These steep sandstone mesas and knob-like formations are as tall as a three-story building and mark Curiosity's gateway to Mount Sharp. Named after Caltech planetary scientist and JPL director Bruce Murray, who pioneered NASA's Mariner and Viking exploration of Mars. Ancient wind-blown dunes petrified into cross-bedded sandstone, eroded over billions of years much like Monument Valley on Earth. Tall butte cliffs can partially block low-elevation UHF passes with Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter and MAVEN overhead. Curiosity arrived at the basal Murray formation in September 2014 and performed a multi-pass geological walkabout just like a field geologist. Finely laminated mudstones formed at the bottom of a long-lived freshwater lake inside Gale Crater 3.5 billion years ago. Drilled samples analyzed by CheMin and SAM revealed hematite, magnetite, jarosite, and changing redox chemistry in the ancient lakebed. Rising 5.5 km above Gale Crater floor, Mount Sharp records Mars' transition from a clay-rich wet era to a sulfate-rich arid climate. In Marias Pass, younger eolian Stimson sandstone sits directly atop older lacustrine Murray mudstone across a sharp geological contact. The Buckskin sample revealed ~74% silica and unexpected tridymite, evidence of evolved silicic volcanism on ancient Mars. Curiosity captured a composite MAHLI selfie here in August 2015 to inspect wheel grouser wear and dust accumulation. Curiosity remained parked here throughout June 2015 while Mars passed behind the Sun relative to Earth. Erosion-resistant ridge rich in crystalline red hematite where Curiosity tested percussive feed-extended drilling techniques. In 2024 Curiosity crushed a white rock in Gediz Vallis channel and discovered pure elemental sulfur crystals for the first time on Mars. Polygonal mineral-filled fracture ridges formed by ancient groundwater circulating through bedrock during the waning stages of habitable Mars. Perseverance touched down on February 18, 2021 at 18.38°N, 77.58°E inside Jezero Crater using Terrain-Relative Navigation (TRN) to avoid hazardous cliffs and dunes. PIXL and SHERLOC revealed that the crater floor consists of olivine cumulate (Seitah) and basaltic lava flows (Maaz) altered by liquid water. Steeply dipping foreset beds topped by horizontal topset boulder conglomerates confirm a stable ancient lake level and high-energy floods in Jezero. Between Sol 623 and 653, Perseverance deposited 10 sealed titanium sample tubes in a precise zigzag pattern as the backup cache for Mars Sample Return. Includes cores from Bearwallow, Skyland, Malay, Coulettes, plus a regolith sample (Atmo Mountain) and witness tube. Immediately behind Three Forks rises the 40-meter escarpment of the ancient Jezero river-lake delta. In July 2024 (Sol 1215), Perseverance discovered millimeter-scale whitish reaction halos rimmed with iron-phosphate (vivianite) and iron-sulfide (greigite). SHERLOC detected organic carbon molecules alongside calcium sulfate veins and olivine inside the Bright Angel mudstone unit. Ancient river canyon that breached the western rim of Jezero Crater and fed water and sediment into the paleolake. On April 19, 2021 (Sol 58), Ingenuity achieved the first powered, controlled aerodynamic flight on another planet, hovering at 3 meters altitude for 39.1 seconds. Ingenuity flew 625 meters at 5 m/s across the sandy ridges of South Seitah, scouting geological targets ahead of Perseverance. After 72 flights, 17.0 km of distance, and 128.8 minutes airborne, Ingenuity suffered rotor-tip contact on bland sand ripples in Neretva Vallis in January 2024. On January 25, 2004, Opportunity rolled inside the 22-meter Eagle Crater in Meridiani Planum, directly in front of an exposed bedrock outcrop. Mössbauer and Mini-TES confirmed these millimeter-sized gray concretions are crystalline hematite precipitated by ancient acidic groundwater. Festoon cross-lamination and hydrated iron-sulfate jarosite proved liquid water once soaked and flowed across Meridiani Planum. At the rim of the 22-km Endeavour Crater, Opportunity discovered the bright Homestake vein of pure hydrated calcium sulfate (gypsum). Between Sol 3950 and 4480, Opportunity explored clay-bearing Noachian rocks formed under neutral, life-friendly pH waters. Opportunity's final resting place after setting the off-Earth driving record of 45.16 km before the global dust storm of June 2018 (Sol 5111). An 80-meter layered plateau of volcaniclastic deposits in the Inner Basin of the Columbia Hills inside Gusev Crater. On Sol 1148, Spirit's immobilized right-front wheel trenched bright white soil containing 90% opaline silica—smoking-gun evidence of ancient hot springs or fumaroles. Mössbauer and Mini-TES identified 16–34% magnesium-iron carbonate in the Comanche outcrop, proving non-acidic hydrothermal activity. On May 14, 2021, CNSA's Tianwen-1 lander and Zhurong rover touched down at 25.066°N, 109.925°E in southern Utopia Planitia. Zhurong's dual-frequency ground-penetrating radar imaged two graded flood layers down to 80 m depth and buried paleo-polygonal wedges at 35 m depth. MarSCoDe laser spectroscopy detected hydrated sulfates, opaline silica, and iron oxides on cemented transverse eolian ridges from modern frost thaw. Sojourner deployed its Alpha Proton X-Ray Spectrometer (APXS) against Barnacle Bill (silica-rich andesite) and Yogi (vesicular basalt). Mars Pathfinder bounced to a halt in airbags on July 4, 1997, opened its three triangular solar petals, and deployed Sojourner down its metal ramps. Imbricated boulders and streamlined hills visible on the horizon confirm Ares Vallis was carved by megafloods discharging into Chryse Planitia. Viking 1 Lander touched down at 22.27°N, 312.05°E in Chryse Planitia and operated continuously for over 6 Earth years (2,245 Sols). The 3-meter collector boom dug trenches into drift soil; Labeled Release detected 14CO2 gas evolution driven by reactive soil superoxides and perchlorates. A 2-meter vesicular basalt block sitting 8 meters from the lander, surrounded by wind-sculpted fine regolith drifts. At 47.64°N in Utopia Planitia, Viking 2 photographed a bright white blanket of water-ice and CO2 frost coating the rocks during two Martian winters. Gas-bubble pitted boulders and shallow thermal-contraction troughs cover the flat plain around Gerald Soffen Memorial Station. Viking 2 used its sampler arm to nudge aside rocks and collect subsurface soil shielded from solar ultraviolet radiation. Phoenix's 2.35-m robotic arm scraped away 5 cm of arctic soil at 68.22°N, exposing bright water ice cubes that sublimated over four sols. The MECA wet chemistry lab and TEGA thermal ovens discovered perchlorate salts (ClO4-), alkaline pH (7.7), and 3–5% calcium carbonate. On Sol 99, the zenith-pointing green laser lidar detected water-ice snow crystals falling from cirrus clouds 4 km above the arctic plains. Placed directly on the ground under its white dome shield, SEIS detected 1,319 marsquakes including magnitude 4.7 event S1222a and measured Mars' 1,830-km liquid iron-alloy core. The German HP3 percussion probe encountered an unexpectedly cemented, low-friction duricrust layer inside Homestead Hollow; the IDA scoop assisted in burying the mole. A 27-meter quasi-circular impact hollow filled with sandy regolith in western Elysium Planitia (4.5024°N, 135.6234°E). The 2.1-meter robotic arm deploys a rotary-percussive drill, dust removal brush, microscopic imager, and X-ray/UV spectrometers directly onto Martian rocks. Houses stereo Navigation Cameras, high-resolution Mastcams, and laser spectroscopy (ChemCam / SuperCam) that vaporizes rock up to 7 meters away. Inside the warm electronics box sit analytical laboratories and sample handling systems that decode Martian mineralogy and organic chemistry. The Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator converts heat from plutonium-238 dioxide into continuous electrical power day and night. Transmits high-volume science telemetry and stereo imagery to Mars orbiters (MRO, Odyssey, MAVEN, TGO) for relay to the Deep Space Network. Six independently driven 50-cm aluminum wheels on a titanium rocker-bogie suspension allow the rover to climb 30-degree slopes and rocks.
